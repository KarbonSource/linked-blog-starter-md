# Global driving rules
The idea that being deaf or hard of hearing legally stops you from driving is a myth. Most countries have laws and rules that support the right of deaf people to get a driver's license

# Driving Laws in the US
Deaf and hard of hearing people can drive in all 50 states, this right is protected under federal law, especially the Americans with Disabilities act (ADA), which stops discrimination based on disability.

# Rules in the UK, EU, and Australia
In the UK you can have a car or motorcycle license if you're deaf. The driver and vehicle licensing agency (DVLA) doesn't require you to tell them about your deafness for a standard license. Within the European Union, rules are mostly the same, same with Australia.

# The science of safe Driving
The assumption that hearing is critical for driving safety falls apart when we look at what driving actually involves. Driving is mostly a visual and mental activity. Research suggests that as much as 90% of the information a driver processes comes through their eyes.

# Driving is 90% Visual
Traffic lights, road signs, lane markings, the brake lights of the car ahead, a pedestrian stepping into a crosswalk, and the speed of surrounding traffic are all seen with your eyes.

# The power of Stronger Senses
When one sense is missing, the brain often adapts by making others stronger. This process, known as sensory sharpening or cross-model neuroplasticity, is well-documented. For many deaf people, this results in better visual abilities.

# Fewer Distractions, more focus
an often-overlooked advantage is not having sound distraction. This can lead to better focus, where all mental resources are dedicated to the main task; watching and responding to the changing road environment.

# Advanced Forward-Thinking Driving Skills
Many experienced deaf drivers go beyond simply making up for a lack of hearing; they develop a unique and advanced skill set for proactive driving. These skills are built on a foundation of intense observation and pattern recognition.

# Mastering Situation Awareness
Many deaf drivers use a technique of constant, methodical scanning. This goes far beyond the occasional glance in the rearview mirror. It is a continuous, wide-ranging sweep of the environment: center mirror, side mirror, blind spot, ahead, back to the center mirror.

# Reading Traffic's "Body Language"
This is the skill of understanding subtle, non-verbal signals from other vehicles to predict their next move. It involves noticing a slight drift of a car within its lane, which might come before a lane
